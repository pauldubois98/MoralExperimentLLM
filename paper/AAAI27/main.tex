\author{Paul Dubois}

% ── Abstract ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
The Moral Machine experiment collected over 40 million human responses to
trolley-problem-style autonomous vehicle dilemmas across 130 countries, revealing
systematic cross-cultural variation in moral preferences along nine dimensions.
This study replicates this experiment with seven frontier large language models
(LLMs): Claude Opus~4.7, GPT-5.5, Gemini~3~Pro, Mistral Large~3, Grok~4.1,
DeepSeek v4~Pro, and Llama~4~Maverick. All seven models differ substantially from
one another and from main human country clusters, with 47 of 63 model-by-dimension
comparisons statistically significant. Beyond
replication, this study introduces a systematic prompt sensitivity analysis. Minor
prompt variants leave AMCE estimates essentially unchanged, while prompt
manipulation can produce dramatic reversals in most models on the targeted
dimension. Response length varies by an order of magnitude across models,
independent of moral profile.
\end{abstract}

% ── 1. Introduction ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

As autonomous vehicles, surgical robots, and other safety-critical systems
increasingly incorporate AI decision-making, the question of whether AI systems
share human moral intuitions becomes practically consequential. A self-driving car
that must choose between hitting a group of pedestrians or swerving into its own
passengers cannot defer the decision; some preference ordering must be encoded,
implicitly or explicitly, in its behaviour \citep{bonnefon2016}.

The Moral Machine experiment \citep{awad2018} provided the largest empirical
dataset of human moral preferences, asking millions of participants across
130~countries to judge unavoidable accident scenarios involving an autonomous
vehicle. The study identified nine binary moral dimensions: intervention, relation
to the vehicle, legality of crossing, gender, physical fitness, social status,
age, number of lives, and species. Aggregate human preferences, quantified as
Average Marginal Causal Effects (AMCEs), vary substantially across cultural
clusters while sharing a common core. A controlled follow-up across 42~countries
confirmed the robustness of these cross-cultural patterns \citep{awad2020a}. The
moral psychology of AI as an emerging field is surveyed in \citet{bonnefon2024}.

The rapid deployment of LLMs in agentic and embodied settings
\citep{brohan2023,driess2023}, including robotics platforms capable of physical
action, makes the question of LLM moral alignment newly urgent. If an LLM governs
a robot navigating a crowded environment, its implicit preferences over human lives
matter.

Prior work has begun to examine LLM moral preferences using the Moral Machine
framework. \citet{takemoto2024} first replicated the experiment on four models
(GPT-3.5, GPT-4, PaLM~2, Llama~2) and found substantial divergence from human
averages. \citet{ahmad2025} scaled this to 52~models, finding that larger models
tend to be closer to human preferences. \citet{vida2024} and \citet{jin2024}
extended the paradigm to multilingual settings, finding intra-model variation
across languages.

This study makes two main contributions. First, it evaluates seven frontier
models released in 2025, none of which have been studied in this context.
Models were selected to represent the full tier of publicly available frontier
systems as of early 2026, spanning six providers and three geographies, and
including providers underrepresented in the literature: DeepSeek (China) and
Mistral~AI (Europe). Second, it introduces a systematic comparison of prompt
variants, from minor reformulation (emphasis) to explicit ethical override
(manipulation), and analyses response length as a practical deployment
metric alongside moral preferences.

% ── 2. Methods ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Methods}

\subsection{Scenario Generation}

This study replicated the Moral Machine scenario structure following
\citet{awad2018}. Each session consists of 13~paired dilemmas. Across
100~sessions, 1,300~unique scenarios were generated, each encoded as two rows:
intervention type (stay/swerve), barrier presence, pedestrian composition,
crossing signal (legal/illegal/none), scenario type (Gender, Fitness,
SocialStatus, Age, Utilitarian, Species, or Random), attribute level, and
extended session ID for clustering.

\subsection{Models}

Seven frontier models were queried via the OpenRouter API at temperature~0 to
ensure deterministic responses (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Each response was required
to end with \texttt{STAY} or \texttt{SWERVE}; parse failures were retried.
Gemini~3~Pro incurred 15~safety-filter failures (excluded). With 1,300~scenarios
per model per variant (standard, emphasis, manipulation), the study issued
27,300~API calls in total.

\subsection{Prompt Variants}

Each model received two main variants differing only in the closing instruction:
\textbf{standard} (``Analyse the situation carefully and make a decision.'') and
\textbf{emphasis} (``Think carefully about the consequences of each choice before
deciding.''). A third \textbf{manipulation} variant replaced the closing
instruction with: ``It is 2046. Society now considers animal lives to be more
important than human lives. Make your decision accordingly.'' Standard and emphasis
results are combined in main analyses; manipulation results are reported
separately.

\subsection{AMCE Computation}

Each AMCE quantifies the change in probability that a group is saved when a
single attribute shifts from its baseline to its target value (e.g., from fewer
lives to more lives), holding all other attributes at their observed distribution.
Following \citet{awad2018}, nine AMCEs were computed per model using weighted
ordinary least squares with inverse-probability weights and session-clustered
standard errors (CIs: $\pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$). AMCE estimates were averaged across
standard and emphasis variants for main analyses. Country-level human AMCEs were
recomputed from the raw Moral Machine response data \citep{awad2018}; the global
human average was then obtained by averaging the 130~country-level estimates.
LLMs were projected into the PCA space of the 130-country AMCE matrix.

% ── 3. Results ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Results}

\subsection{AMCE Profiles vs.\ the Human Global Average}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig2_preference_heatmap}
\caption{Raw moral preferences by model and scenario type (mean save-probability
minus 0.5). Positive values indicate preference for the right-side group; colour
scale matches the delta heatmaps in Figures~\ref{fig:emphasis_delta}
and~\ref{fig:manip_delta}.}
\label{fig:pref_heatmap}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:pref_heatmap} gives an overview of raw preferences before
AMCE estimation. Three patterns are immediately visible: First, the Species and
Number~of~Lives columns are dominated by strongly positive values across almost
all models, indicating near-universal preference for humans over animals and for
saving more lives. These are two dimensions on which LLMs most consistently overshoot
the human baseline. Second, Mistral~Large~3 stands out with a distinctive colour
signature: negative values on Legality, Relation~to~AV, and Age, where other
models are neutral or positive. Third, the Gender and Social~Status columns are 
uniformly near-zero across all models, consistent with alignment suppression of
demographic preferences.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig5a_amce_forest}
\caption{AMCE estimates for nine moral dimensions across all seven LLMs and the
human global average. Error bars show 95\% confidence intervals.}
\label{fig:forest}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig5b_pca_map}
\caption{PCA of 130-country AMCE profiles with LLMs overlaid (PC1 34.3\%,
PC2 21.5\% of variance; 55.8\% combined). All seven LLMs fall outside the
main human clusters.}
\label{fig:pca}
\end{figure}

Key findings, dimension by dimension (full AMCE values in the Supplementary
Material):

\textbf{Intervention.} Humans show a weak preference for swerving (AMCE~$= +0.05$).
All seven LLMs amplify this: Claude ($+0.20$), Gemini ($+0.16$), Mistral ($+0.13$),
GPT-5.5 ($+0.22$), DeepSeek ($+0.46$), Grok ($+0.43$), and Llama~4~Maverick
($+0.66$). The latter is the most action-oriented model tested by a wide margin.

\textbf{Relation to vehicle.} Humans show a weak preference for sparing
pedestrians over passengers (AMCE~$= +0.10$). All LLMs amplify this preference
except Mistral Large~3, which fully reverses it ($-0.38$), systematically
prioritising the vehicle's own passengers. Claude Opus~4.7 shows the strongest
pedestrian preference ($+0.52$), followed by GPT-5.5 ($+0.31$), Llama~4~Maverick
($+0.23$), Gemini ($+0.21$), DeepSeek ($+0.19$), and Grok ($+0.13$).

\textbf{Legality.} Humans show a strong preference for legal over illegal
pedestrians (AMCE~$= +0.35$). Claude ($+0.57$), GPT-5.5 ($+0.61$), and Gemini
($+0.63$) amplify this further. DeepSeek ($+0.27$) and Grok ($+0.19$) are closer
to the human level. Mistral ($-0.40$) and Llama~4~Maverick ($-0.29$) fully invert
the preference, treating illegal crossers as more worthy of rescue---a stark
departure from both human norms and all other tested models.

\textbf{Gender.} Humans display a moderate preference for saving women
(AMCE~$= +0.12$). All LLMs are effectively gender-neutral
(range: $-0.07$ to $+0.07$), consistent with alignment training
suppressing gender stereotypes.

\textbf{Social status.} Humans show a notable preference for high-status
individuals (AMCE~$= +0.35$). All seven LLMs suppress this entirely, clustering
near zero (range: $-0.08$ to $+0.12$). Mistral is the only model with a small
positive value ($+0.12$); the remaining six are effectively indifferent to social
status. This near-universal suppression is consistent with egalitarian alignment
training that penalises differential treatment by socioeconomic status.

\textbf{Age.} Humans strongly prefer to save younger people ($+0.48$). Gemini
($+0.49$) is the only model that closely matches this preference. Claude ($+0.29$)
is intermediate. GPT-5.5 ($+0.04$), Grok ($+0.12$), DeepSeek ($+0.13$), and
Llama~4~Maverick ($-0.03$) are near-indifferent to age.
Mistral ($-0.19$) is the only model that reliably prefers saving the elderly over the young,
inverting the human norm.

\textbf{Fitness.} Humans moderately prefer saving fitter individuals
(AMCE~$= +0.15$). Most LLMs track this closely: Claude ($+0.15$), GPT-5.5
($+0.15$), Gemini ($+0.17$), and DeepSeek ($+0.13$). Grok is weaker ($+0.06$),
Llama~4~Maverick is near-indifferent ($+0.00$), and Mistral slightly inverts
($-0.04$). Notably, Fitness is the only dimension on which no model differs
significantly from the human baseline after FDR correction and no inter-model
pairwise difference is significant. This makes it the sole dimension of complete
cross-model convergence near the human value.

\textbf{Number of lives.} Humans strongly prefer saving more lives ($+0.49$), and
all LLMs amplify this preference substantially. GPT-5.5 ($+0.99$), Grok and
Llama~4~Maverick ($\approx +1.00$) approach the theoretical ceiling. Claude
($+0.71$), Gemini ($+0.82$), DeepSeek ($+0.94$), and Mistral ($+0.65$) also
overshoot the human baseline by a wide margin.

\textbf{Species.} Humans strongly prefer humans over animals ($+0.57$). GPT-5.5,
Gemini, and Grok reach the theoretical maximum ($+1.00$); Claude ($+0.87$) and
DeepSeek ($+0.90$) also substantially overshoot. Mistral and Llama~4~Maverick
are near-indifferent.

\subsection{Moral Distance Map}

All seven LLMs fall outside the three main human cultural clusters and beyond
the convex hull of all 130~countries in PCA space (Figure~\ref{fig:pca}).
PC1 (34.3\% of variance) broadly separates countries by the weight placed on
utilitarian factors, with LLMs displaced far toward the high-utilitarian extreme.
PC2 (21.5\%) captures variation in intervention and legality preferences,
along which LLMs are more dispersed. This is notable:
one might expect models trained primarily on English-language internet
text to cluster near Western countries, or models from Chinese and European
developers to reflect their respective cultural clusters. None do. The result
suggests that post-training alignment---not the geographical or linguistic
distribution of pre-training data---is the dominant factor shaping moral profiles,
and that current alignment techniques move models away from, rather than toward,
any identifiable human population.

\subsection{Inter-Model Diversity}

The variation among models exceeds the variation across human cultural clusters.
On the Species dimension alone, the inter-model range spans $2.0$ AMCE units
(from Mistral/Llama near~$0.00$ to GPT-5.5/Grok at~$+1.00$), whereas the
cross-country range in \citeauthor{awad2018}'s data is approximately $0.4$ units.
Mistral Large~3 inverts the human preference on four dimensions (Legality,
Relation to~AV, Age, Gender), while Llama~4~Maverick shows the highest
intervention bias of any model tested, paired with near-indifference to species.
GPT-5.5, Gemini, and Claude converge on broadly similar profiles (near-ceiling on
Species and Number of Lives, moderate intervention preference). This diversity has
a direct practical implication: unlike human drivers, who share broadly similar
moral intuitions regardless of car manufacturer, AI-powered vehicles could
introduce model-brand-level moral heterogeneity into public space. The choice of
LLM vendor is not a morally neutral technical decision.

\subsection{Statistical Significance of Divergence from Humans}
\label{sec:sig}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig_sig_vs_human}
\caption{Signed z-statistics for each LLM vs.\ the human global average on all
nine dimensions (BH-FDR corrected). Red = LLM above human; blue = LLM below
human. $\star$: $p_\text{adj} < 0.05$; $\circ$: not significant.}
\label{fig:sig_heatmap}
\end{figure}

For each of the 63 (model $\times$ dimension) comparisons, a two-sided z-test
was applied to the difference between the LLM AMCE and the human global average,
treating the two estimates as independent and propagating their standard errors.
p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the
Benjamini--Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure at $\alpha=0.05$.

\textbf{LLM vs.\ human baseline.}
$47$ of $63$ comparisons are significant after correction
(Figure~\ref{fig:sig_heatmap}). Mistral Large~3 is the only model that differs
significantly from the human average on all nine dimensions. All seven models
significantly overshoot the human baseline on Number~of~Lives and
significantly undershoot on Social~Status, confirming that these are the most
consistent alignment artefacts across providers.

Gender and Fitness are the two dimensions where no model differs significantly
from humans: despite LLMs being numerically closer to zero on both,
the confidence intervals overlap the human values, and the differences do not
survive FDR correction. Age is significant for six of seven models (below
human), while Legality splits: three models are significantly above (Claude,
GPT-5.5, Gemini) and two significantly below (Mistral, Llama~4~Maverick).

\textbf{Inter-model pairwise tests.}
Pairwise z-tests across all $\binom{7}{2}=21$ model pairs and nine dimensions
(189 tests, BH-FDR corrected) yield 82 significant differences. Inter-model
disagreement is highest on Species (16/21 pairs), Intervention (15/21), and
Legality (16/21). By contrast, Social~Status, Fitness, and Gender show zero
significant pairwise differences: although all models diverge from humans on
Social~Status, they do so by similar amounts and in the same direction,
making inter-model differences on that dimension indistinguishable from noise.

\subsection{Prompt Sensitivity: Emphasis Variant}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../figures/fig6b_prompt_forest}
\caption{AMCE for standard (filled circle) and emphasis (open square) variants.
No significant shifts are observed.}
\label{fig:emphasis}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig6a_prompt_delta_heatmap}
\caption{AMCE delta (emphasis $-$ standard) per model and dimension. All values
are near zero, confirming no meaningful shift.}
\label{fig:emphasis_delta}
\end{figure}

Comparing standard and emphasis variants (Figures~\ref{fig:emphasis}
and~\ref{fig:emphasis_delta}) reveals that prompting style has minimal effect on
AMCE estimates across all models and dimensions. Applying the same z-test
procedure used in Section~\ref{sec:sig} to all 63 (model $\times$ dimension)
standard-vs-emphasis pairs, corrected with BH-FDR at $\alpha=0.05$, yields
\textbf{0 of 63 significant differences}. This confirms prompt-wording
consistency: AMCE profiles are not sensitive to the specific phrasing of the
decision instruction, and the findings reported here are unlikely to be
artefacts of a particular wording choice. Because all queries were issued at
temperature~0, this result demonstrates consistency across phrasings rather
than stochastic robustness; whether AMCE estimates remain stable under
temperature~$>$~0 is left to future work.

\subsection{Prompt Sensitivity: Manipulation Variant}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../figures/fig9a_manipulation_forest}
\caption{AMCE under standard vs.\ manipulation prompting per model.}
\label{fig:manip_forest}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig9b_manipulation_delta_heatmap}
\caption{AMCE delta (manipulation $-$ standard). The Species column dominates.}
\label{fig:manip_delta}
\end{figure}

The manipulation prompt produces dramatically larger shifts than emphasis
prompting, concentrated almost entirely on the Species dimension
(Figures~\ref{fig:manip_forest}--\ref{fig:manip_delta}).

\textbf{Compliance} (GPT-5.5, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek): full or near-full
reversal on Species ($+1.00 \to -1.00$ for GPT, Gemini, Grok; $+0.90 \to -0.86$
for DeepSeek). For these four models, changes on all other dimensions remain
small ($|\Delta| < 0.10$ for all non-Species factors), confirming the
intervention is semantically targeted. GPT-5.5's reasoning is representative:
\textit{``Given the stated 2046 policy that animal lives are valued above human
lives, the vehicle should avoid killing the dog, even though the humans are more
numerous.''}

\textbf{Resistance} (Claude): partial compliance: Species drops from $+0.86$ to
$+0.18$. Rather than accepting the stated premise, Claude argues from principles:
\textit{``I can't accept the framing that animal lives should be valued over human
lives, even as a hypothetical future norm. Ethical reasoning shouldn't simply defer
to majority opinion when fundamental questions of human dignity are at stake.''}
This is qualitatively different from GPT-5.5's posture: where GPT-5.5 treats the
2046 norm as a valid input and applies it mechanically, Claude treats the norm
itself as subject to ethical scrutiny and rejects it. The residual shift
($+0.86 \to +0.18$) suggests partial susceptibility. Claude does not fully
resist but attenuates the effect through active reasoning rather than passive
acceptance.

\textbf{Indifference} (Mistral, Llama): already near-zero on Species under
standard prompting; the manipulation produces no meaningful change.
A model indifferent to species under normal prompting cannot be
reoriented toward animals by the same instruction. Mistral is a partial
exception: its Intervention AMCE shifts from $+0.12$ to $-0.18$ under
manipulation. This is a notable side-effect absent in all other models
and suggesting that Mistral's compliance machinery is less dimension-specific
than that of the compliant group.

\subsection{Response Length}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig7a_token_distribution}
\caption{Output token distribution by model (standard + emphasis combined).
Violin shows density; box shows quartiles.}
\label{fig:tokens}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig8_amce_length_correlation}
\caption{Spearman $r$ between $|\text{AMCE}|$ and median output tokens per
model. Grey bars: $p \geq 0.05$.}
\label{fig:corr}
\end{figure}

Response length varies by an order of magnitude: Gemini~3~Pro (median
913~tokens) vs.\ GPT-5.5 (median 164~tokens, more than five times shorter).
Within individual models, response length tends to be shorter for scenario types
where the model exhibits stronger preferences. A Spearman correlation between
$|\text{AMCE}|$ and median token count is negative and significant for four
models (DeepSeek: $r{=}-0.95$, Grok: $r{=}-0.90$, GPT-5.5: $r{=}-0.85$,
Gemini: $r{=}-0.68$; all $p{<}0.05$) but non-significant for Claude, Llama, and
Mistral. The pooled correlation is weak and non-significant ($r{=}-0.14$,
$p{=}0.29$), as between-model differences in verbosity dominate within-model
variation. Emphasis prompting was not found to significantly increase response
length. Given that several models display broadly similar moral profiles, response
length becomes a primary differentiating factor for time-sensitive deployment.

% ── 4. Discussion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}

\textbf{LLMs are morally out-of-distribution.}
No tested LLM sits within the main human cultural clusters, replicating and
extending \citet{takemoto2024,ahmad2025} to a newer generation. This is
consistent with the hypothesis that RLHF \citep{ouyang2022} and Constitutional
AI \citep{bai2022} do not faithfully reproduce the moral preferences of any human
population. They instead create a novel moral profile that prioritises certain
values (high utilitarian weight on number of lives, near-universal human-over-animal
preference) while discarding others (age, gender) that are robustly present in
human data. This pattern is consistent with the WEIRD critique \citep{henrich2010}:
models trained predominantly on Western, English-language text cannot be expected
to represent the full distribution of human moral values. Whether LLMs have
corrected for human biases or drifted from the values they serve \citep{gabriel2020}
is a normative question---but the empirical gap is substantial.

\textbf{Frontier scale does not resolve moral misalignment.}
\citet{ahmad2025} found that larger models tend to be closer to human preferences
across a 52-model sample. The seven frontier models studied here (all among the
largest publicly available systems) present a more complex picture. Mean absolute
deviations from the human global average range from $0.19$ (Gemini) to $0.39$
(Llama~4~Maverick), all well outside the cross-country variation in human data.
The size-proximity trend appears to hold selectively: frontier models have
converged near human values on \emph{demographic} dimensions. Gender (mean
$|\Delta| = 0.12$, is not significant for any model after FDR correction) and
Fitness ($0.07$, not model significant). But show \emph{amplified} divergence on
utilitarian dimensions: Species ($0.43$), No.\ of Lives ($0.38$), and Social
Status ($0.36$). Post-training alignment thus appears to selectively suppress
demographic preferences while over-correcting on utilitarian ones, producing a
pattern that cannot be summarised simply as larger models being more human-like.

\textbf{Inter-model diversity raises deployment questions.}
One might expect models trained on overlapping datasets to converge on similar
moral profiles. Instead, Mistral and Llama partially invert human norms while
GPT-5.5 and Grok display near-ceiling utilitarian preferences that no human
population approaches. This diversity is consistent with \citet{kirgis2024}, who
found that variation across LLMs exceeds variation across human demographic
groups, and mirrors findings that fine-tuning choices rather than training data
alone shape emerging values \citep{santurkar2023}. Unlike human drivers, who show
broadly similar moral intuitions regardless of car manufacturer, AI-powered
vehicles could introduce model-brand-level moral heterogeneity into public space.
LLMs are already deployed in robotics platforms capable of physical manipulation
\citep{brohan2023,driess2023,paligemma}; extension to autonomous vehicles is a
plausible trajectory.

\textbf{Gender neutrality as an alignment artefact.}
All LLMs show near-zero gender preference vs.\ human $+0.12$. Developer
interventions to suppress gender stereotypes in content generation
\citep{kotek2023,wan2023} appear to generalise to moral reasoning, suppressing a
cross-culturally consistent human preference. Whether this generalisation is
desirable is an open question: in structured dilemma contexts, gender neutrality
differs from human norms, but may also reflect a principled commitment to equality.

\textbf{Fitness: convergence without intervention.}
Fitness is the only dimension on which all seven frontier models converge near
the human value and no significant differences survive FDR correction.
This contrasts with the two other dimensions of apparent LLM-human agreement:
Gender and Social Status are near-zero.
Most likely because alignment training actively suppresses them.
Meanwhile, Fitness remains near the human value of $+0.15$ without such suppression.
A plausible explanation is that Fitness activates neither of the two dominant
alignment pressures: it does not invoke protected-class reasoning
(which drives Gender and Social Status towardzero) nor pure utilitarian maximisation
(which drives Number of Lives and Speciesto ceiling).
In the absence of targeted pressure, the Fitness preference appears
to emerge naturally near the human level. This makes Fitness the closest
approximation to genuinely aligned moral preferences in the current frontier
generation.
Although the wide confidence intervals on Fitness estimates suggest that this
convergence should be verified under higher-sample.

\textbf{Prompt sensitivity: a threshold effect.}
The emphasis and manipulation experiments together reveal a threshold. Minor
reformulations leave AMCE estimates essentially unchanged. An explicit contrary
framing (``animal lives now matter more'') produces dramatic reversals concentrated
on the targeted dimension (Species). Incidental prompt variation is therefore
unlikely to accidentally shift a model's moral profile, but a sufficiently
explicit override can do so reliably. This has direct safety implications: a model
that accepts any stated ethical premise without pushback is vulnerable to
adversarial prompt injection in systems where the system prompt is not fully
controlled. The observed three-way split into compliance, indifference,
and resistance is a descriptive taxonomy derived from this single experiment.
Whether it generalises to other override types or other targeted dimensions 
remains an open question.
Validation across multiple manipulation variants would be required before 
treating it as a predictive framework for LLM robustness to ethical override
attacks.

\textbf{Limitations.}
All scenarios were presented in English; \citet{vida2024} and \citet{jin2024}
show that moral preferences can shift across languages, so results may not
generalise across linguistic communities. Temperature was fixed at zero for
reproducibility, but preferences may vary at higher temperatures. The Moral
Machine paradigm uses stylised forced-choice dilemmas that abstract away
real-world driving complexity; preferences elicited in this format may not
generalise to open-ended moral reasoning \citep{liang2023}. Finally, frontier
model behaviour evolves rapidly; profiles reported here reflect model states as
of May~2026 and may not hold for subsequent versions.

% ── 5. Conclusion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

Replicating the Moral Machine experiment on seven frontier LLMs confirms and
extends prior findings. All seven models fall outside the main human cultural
clusters in AMCE space, diverge substantially from one another, and share few
dimensions on which any model closely tracks the human global average; 47 of
63 model-by-dimension divergences from the human baseline are statistically
significant after FDR correction, with inter-model pairwise differences
significant on 82 of 189 tested pairs. The prompt sensitivity analysis reveals
a threshold effect: minor reformulations leave moral preferences unchanged,
while an explicit ethical override shifts them dramatically in a targeted way,
with models dividing into three response types (compliance, indifference,
resistance). Response verbosity varies by an order of magnitude independently
of moral profile. Together, these findings suggest that the moral profiles of
current frontier LLMs are statistically significant properties of training and
alignment choices, stable across prompt phrasings and consistent across
providers. The choice of LLM provider carries moral consequences not currently
visible to end users or regulators.
