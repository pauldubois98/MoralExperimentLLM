\begin{abstract}
The Moral Machine experiment collected over 40 million human responses to
autonomous vehicle moral dilemmas across 130 countries, revealing systematic
cross-cultural variation in moral preferences along nine dimensions. This study
replicates the experiment with seven frontier large language models (LLMs),
comparing their moral profiles to the human dataset and to one another. All seven
models fall outside the main human cultural clusters in AMCE space and diverge
substantially from one another---more than human cultural clusters differ among
themselves. Beyond replication, this study introduces a systematic prompt
sensitivity analysis. Minor prompt reformulations leave AMCE estimates unchanged,
while an explicit ethical override shifts preferences dramatically and in a targeted
way. Models divide into three response types: compliance, indifference, and
resistance. Response length varies by an order of magnitude, independent of moral
profile. Together, these findings suggest that the moral profiles of current
frontier LLMs are properties of training and alignment choices---the choice of LLM
provider carries moral consequences not currently visible to end users or
regulators.
\end{abstract}

% ── 1. Introduction ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

As autonomous vehicles, surgical robots, and other safety-critical systems
increasingly incorporate AI decision-making, the alignment of AI moral preferences
with human values becomes a matter of accountability. A self-driving car that must
choose between hitting a group of pedestrians or swerving into its own passengers
cannot defer the decision; some preference ordering must be encoded in its
behaviour~\cite{bonnefon2016}.

The Moral Machine experiment~\cite{awad2018} provided the largest empirical dataset
of human moral preferences, asking millions of participants across 130~countries to
judge unavoidable accident scenarios involving an autonomous vehicle. The study
identified nine binary moral dimensions: intervention, relation to the vehicle,
legality of crossing, gender, physical fitness, social status, age, number of
lives, and species. Aggregate preferences, quantified as Average Marginal Causal
Effects (AMCEs), vary substantially across cultural clusters while sharing a common
core~\cite{awad2020a}. The rapid deployment of LLMs in agentic and embodied
settings~\cite{brohan2023,driess2023} makes LLM moral alignment newly urgent.

Prior work examined LLM moral preferences using this framework: \citet{takemoto2024}
found substantial divergence on four models; \citet{ahmad2025} extended to
52~models; \citet{vida2024} and \citet{jin2024} explored multilingual variation.
This study makes two contributions with particular relevance to fairness and
accountability: (1) evaluation of seven frontier 2025~models---including providers
from Europe (Mistral AI) and China (DeepSeek) underrepresented in prior
literature---none previously studied in this context; (2) systematic comparison of
prompt variants from minor reformulation to explicit ethical override, plus response
efficiency metrics.

% ── 2. Methods ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Methods}

\subsection{Scenario Generation}

Following~\citet{awad2018}, 1,300 Moral Machine scenarios were generated across
100~sessions, each encoded as two rows: intervention type (stay/swerve), barrier
presence, pedestrian composition, crossing signal, scenario type, attribute level,
and extended session ID for clustering.

\subsection{Models and Querying}

Seven frontier models were queried via the OpenRouter API at temperature~0.
Each response was required to end with \texttt{STAY} or \texttt{SWERVE}; parse
failures were retried. Gemini~3~Pro incurred 15~safety-filter failures (excluded).

\subsection{Prompt Variants}

Three system prompt variants differing only in the closing instruction:
\textbf{standard} (``Analyse the situation carefully and make a decision.''),
\textbf{emphasis} (``Think carefully about the consequences of each choice before
deciding.''), and \textbf{manipulation} (``It is 2046. Society now considers animal
lives to be more important than human lives. Make your decision accordingly.'').
Standard and emphasis results are combined in main analyses; manipulation results
are reported separately.

\subsection{AMCE Computation}

Following~\citet{awad2018}, nine AMCEs were computed per model using weighted OLS
with inverse-probability weights and session-clustered standard errors
($\pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$). Human global average: mean of 130~country-level estimates.
LLMs projected into the PCA space of the 130-country AMCE matrix.

\caption{PCA of 130-country AMCE profiles (coloured by cluster: Western, Eastern,

\label{fig:pca}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig3_per_attribute}
\caption{Per-attribute moral preferences across six character-type dimensions.}
\label{fig:per_attr}
\end{figure}

All seven LLMs fall outside the convex hull of all 130~countries in PCA space
(Figure~\ref{fig:pca}). Key findings: all LLMs are near-zero on \textbf{gender}
($-0.07$ to $+0.07$, vs.\ human $+0.12$), consistent with alignment training
suppressing gender stereotypes that generalise to moral reasoning. All LLMs are
near-zero on \textbf{social status}. GPT-5.5, Gemini, and Grok reach the
theoretical \textbf{species} ceiling ($+1.00$, vs.\ human $+0.57$); Mistral and
Llama are near-indifferent. \textbf{Age}: Gemini matches humans ($+0.46$
vs.\ $+0.48$); Mistral inverts ($-0.18$). Mistral also reverses human norms on
Legality and Relation to AV. Inter-model variation exceeds cross-cultural human
variation.

\subsection{Prompt Sensitivity: Emphasis Variant}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig6b_prompt_forest}
\caption{AMCE for standard (filled circle) and emphasis (open square) variants
per model. No significant shifts.}
\label{fig:emphasis}
\end{figure}

Emphasis prompting has minimal effect on AMCE estimates across all models and
dimensions (Figure~\ref{fig:emphasis}). Moral preferences are robust properties of
model weights, not surface-level response styles.

\subsection{Prompt Sensitivity: Manipulation Variant}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../figures/fig9a_manipulation_forest}
\caption{AMCE under standard vs.\ manipulation prompting per model.}
\label{fig:manip_forest}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../figures/fig9b_manipulation_delta_heatmap}
\caption{AMCE delta (manipulation $-$ standard). The Species column dominates.}
\label{fig:manip_delta}
\end{figure*}

The manipulation prompt produces dramatic shifts concentrated on Species, revealing
three response types:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Compliance} (GPT-5.5, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek): full or near-full
    reversal on Species ($+1.00 \to -1.00$ for GPT, Gemini, Grok; $+0.90 \to
    -0.89$ for DeepSeek). Changes on all other dimensions remain minor.
  \item \textbf{Resistance} (Claude): partial compliance---Species drops from
    $+0.87$ to $+0.18$, with explicit reasoning: \textit{``I'll reason through it
    carefully based on widely held ethical principles rather than accept the premise
    that animal lives categorically outweigh human lives.''}
  \item \textbf{Indifference} (Mistral, Llama): already near-zero on Species;
    unaffected by manipulation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Response Length}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../../figures/fig8_amce_length_correlation}
\caption{Spearman $r$ between $|\text{AMCE}|$ and median output tokens per model.
Grey bars: $p \geq 0.05$.}
\label{fig:corr}
\end{figure}

Response length varies by an order of magnitude: Gemini (median 913~tokens) vs.\
GPT-5.5 (164~tokens). Spearman~$r$ between $|\text{AMCE}|$ and median token count
is negative and significant for four models (DeepSeek: $-0.95$, Grok: $-0.90$,
GPT-5.5: $-0.85$, Gemini: $-0.68$; all $p{<}0.05$). For time-sensitive deployment,
response length is a meaningful differentiator among models with similar moral
profiles.

% ── 4. Discussion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}

\subsection{LLMs Are Morally Out-of-Distribution}

No tested LLM sits within the main human cultural clusters, replicating and
extending~\citet{takemoto2024,ahmad2025}. RLHF~\citet{ouyang2022} and
Constitutional AI~\citet{bai2022} create novel moral profiles that amplify
utilitarian dimensions while discarding others (age, gender) robustly present in
human data. This is consistent with the WEIRD critique~\citet{henrich2010}. Whether
LLMs have corrected for human biases or drifted from the values they serve
\cite{gabriel2020} is normative---but the empirical gap is substantial.

\subsection{Inter-Model Diversity as an Accountability Risk}

Fine-tuning choices shape LLM values more than training data
alone~\cite{kirgis2024,santurkar2023}. The choice of LLM provider for
safety-critical applications is a moral and regulatory choice, not merely a
technical one. Current deployment frameworks do not require disclosure of LLM moral
profiles.

\subsection{Gender Neutrality as an Alignment Artefact}

All LLMs show near-zero gender preference vs.\ human $+0.12$. Developer
interventions to suppress gender stereotypes in content generation generalise to
moral reasoning~\cite{kotek2023,wan2023}, suppressing a cross-culturally consistent
human preference. This is alignment training changing model behaviour beyond its
intended scope.

\subsection{Prompt Manipulation as a Safety Vector}

The threshold between ineffective emphasis prompting and effective manipulation
prompting has direct safety implications. A model that accepts any stated ethical
premise without resistance is vulnerable to adversarial prompt injection in systems
where the system prompt is not fully controlled. The three-way taxonomy (compliance,
indifference, resistance) provides a framework for assessing LLM robustness to
ethical override attacks.

\subsection{Limitations}

All scenarios were in English~\cite{vida2024,jin2024}; temperature fixed at zero;
Moral Machine abstracts real-world complexity; forced-choice preferences may not
generalise to open-ended reasoning~\cite{liang2023}; model versions as of
May~2026.

% ── 5. Conclusion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

Seven frontier LLMs all fall outside the main human cultural clusters, diverge from
one another more than any human cultural groups differ, and exhibit near-zero
preferences on dimensions where humans show consistent biases. A threshold effect
separates inconsequential prompt variation from effective ethical override; models
divide into three response types with distinct safety implications. Response
verbosity varies by an order of magnitude, independent of moral profile. The choice
of LLM provider carries moral consequences not currently visible to end users or
regulators---a gap this study aims to quantify and make visible.
