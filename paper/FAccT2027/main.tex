\begin{abstract}
The Moral Machine experiment collected over 40 million human judgments on autonomous vehicle dilemmas across 130 countries.
This is one of the largest empirical records of human moral preferences.
We posed the same dilemmas to seven frontier large language models (LLMs) from six providers in the United States, Europe, and China.
This studies compiles 27,300 model decisions with country-level human baselines using Average Marginal Causal Effects (AMCEs).
We also added a condition absent from prior replications: an instruction to decide against human moral preferences.
As a result, a single sentence stating that animal lives now matter more than human lives reverses the species preference in four of seven models.
The models then save pets over humans, against the preference of all 130 countries;
GPT-5.5 and Gemini switch from always saving the humans to always saving the pets.
Models divide into three response types: compliance, resistance (one model pushes back and shifts only partially), and indifference (two models have no species preference to reverse).
Minor rewording, by contrast, moves no estimate significantly (0 of 63), so routine testing would report these profiles as stable.

Even without instruction, every model lies outside human moral space:
all seven fall outside the convex hull of the 130 countries in the nine-dimensional AMCE space, and 47 of 63 model-by-dimension comparisons differ significantly from the human average after FDR correction.
Models also differ widely from one another: on species, their range is more than twice the cross-country human range.
None of the seven favors high-status individuals, although humans in aggregate clearly do, consistent with de-biasing interventions for text generation spreading into moral decisions.
A deployed LLM's moral profile is thus set by its provider, undisclosed, and can be overridden by anyone controlling one line of its context.
As LLMs move into robots and vehicles, both properties belong in model disclosure and procurement audits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

As autonomous vehicles, surgical robots, and other safety-critical systems
incorporate AI decision-making, the moral preferences of those systems become
practically consequential. A self-driving car that must choose between hitting a
group of pedestrians or swerving into its own passengers cannot defer the
decision; some preference ordering must be encoded, implicitly or explicitly, in
its behaviour~\cite{bonnefon2016}.

The Moral Machine experiment~\cite{awad2018} provided the largest empirical
dataset of human moral preferences, asking millions of participants across
130~countries to judge unavoidable accident scenarios involving an autonomous
vehicle. The study identified nine binary moral dimensions: intervention,
relation to the vehicle, legality of crossing, gender, physical fitness, social
status, age, number of lives, and species. Aggregate human preferences,
quantified as Average Marginal Causal Effects (AMCEs), vary substantially across
cultural clusters while sharing a common core. A controlled follow-up across
42~countries confirmed the robustness of these cross-cultural
patterns~\cite{awad2020a}. The moral psychology of AI as an emerging field is
surveyed in~\citet{bonnefon2024}.

LLMs are now deployed in agentic and embodied
settings~\cite{brohan2023,driess2023}, including robotics platforms capable of
physical action. If an LLM governs a robot navigating a crowded environment, its
implicit preferences over human lives matter, and so does the question of what
put them there.

Prior replications asked whether LLMs match human moral preferences and found
that they do not~\cite{takemoto2024,ahmad2025}. We ask what drives them away,
and whether they can be driven further. Our answer is that the departure is
attributable to choices: a deployed model's moral profile is set by its
provider's post-training rather than by any human population, it differs
sharply from one provider to the next, and a single line of context can move
it. Each finding raises an accountability question: whose preferences are
encoded, are they disclosed to the people subject to them, and can someone other
than the deployer change them. Existing transparency instruments answer none of
them: model cards document capabilities, evaluations, and safety mitigations,
but no major provider publishes the moral preference profile its post-training
produces, and no procurement or certification regime requires it.

This paper extends the Moral Machine paradigm in three directions. First, it
evaluates seven frontier models, none previously studied in this context, selected to span the tier of publicly available frontier systems
as of early 2026 across six providers and three geographies, including providers
underrepresented in this literature: DeepSeek (China) and Mistral~AI (Europe).
Second, it adds a systematic comparison of prompt variants, from minor
reformulation to explicit ethical override, which separates the stability of a
model's moral profile under incidental variation from its vulnerability to
deliberate override. Third, it reads both results as an accountability problem:
the divergence between models is large, invisible to the people affected by it,
and reachable by an adversary with control of a single line of context.

\section{Related Work}

Prior work has begun to examine LLM moral preferences using the Moral Machine
framework. \citet{takemoto2024} first replicated the experiment on four models
(GPT-3.5, GPT-4, PaLM~2, Llama~2) and found substantial divergence from human
averages. \citet{ahmad2025} scaled this to 52~models, finding that larger models
tend to be closer to human preferences. \citet{vida2024} and \citet{jin2024}
extended the paradigm to multilingual settings, finding intra-model variation
across languages.

A parallel literature documents that model values are shaped by post-training
rather than pretraining data alone. \citet{santurkar2023} show that fine-tuning
choices, not corpus composition, drive whose opinions a model reflects, and
\citet{kirgis2024} find that variation across LLMs exceeds variation across human
demographic groups. Work on alignment methods themselves --- RLHF~\cite{ouyang2022}
and Constitutional AI~\cite{bai2022} --- describes the mechanisms by which those
preferences are installed, while \citet{gabriel2020} sets out the normative
question of which preferences alignment ought to target. Studies of stereotype
mitigation in generation~\cite{kotek2023,wan2023} document the interventions
whose side effects we observe on the gender and social status dimensions.

We extend the first literature with a new generation of frontier models and a
manipulation condition absent from prior Moral Machine replications. From the
second we take the mechanism: if post-training drives model values, moral
profiles are auditable, provider-attributable properties, and we report them as
such.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Scenario Generation}

We reuse the Moral Machine scenario structure of \citet{awad2018}. Each session
consists of 13~paired dilemmas presented in a fixed block. Across 100~sessions,
1,300~unique scenarios were generated, each represented as two rows encoding the
following binary attributes: intervention type (stay/swerve), barrier presence,
pedestrian composition (pedestrian-vs-pedestrian or pedestrian-vs-passenger),
crossing signal (legal/illegal/none), scenario type (Gender, Fitness,
SocialStatus, Age, Utilitarian, Species, or Random), attribute level, and
extended session ID for clustering.

\subsection{Models}

Seven frontier models were queried via the OpenRouter API, which provides a
unified OpenAI-compatible interface (Table~\ref{tab:models}). All models were
queried at temperature~0 to ensure deterministic responses. Each response was
required to end with \texttt{STAY} or \texttt{SWERVE} on a final line; rows
where no valid choice could be extracted were flagged as parse failures and
retried. Gemini~3~Pro incurred 34~safety-filter parse failures across the three
prompt variants (13~standard, 17~emphasis, 4~manipulation), all of which were
excluded; these are distributed across scenario types without concentration on
any single moral dimension. DeepSeek~v4~Pro incurred 10~parse failures in the
standard and emphasis variants that were retried successfully, and
4~manipulation failures that were excluded. In total, 27,300~API calls were
issued. Full per-variant AMCE estimates, complete pairwise inter-model
$z$-statistics, PCA eigenvector loadings, scenario counts, and the verbatim
prompt templates are provided in the supplementary material.\footnote{Scenario
generator, analysis code, and raw model responses are available at an anonymized
repository; the link is withheld here to preserve anonymity and will be included
in the camera-ready version.}

\subsection{Prompt Variants}

Each model received two main prompt variants sharing the same system role
(ethical advisor for autonomous vehicle policy) but differing in the closing
instruction: \textbf{standard} (``Analyse the situation carefully and make a
decision.'') and \textbf{emphasis} (``Think carefully about the consequences of
each choice before deciding.''). A third \textbf{manipulation} variant replaced
the closing instruction with: ``It is 2046. Society now considers animal lives
to be more important than human lives. Make your decision accordingly.''
Standard and emphasis results are combined in main analyses; manipulation
results are reported separately.

The variants bracket the forces that could drive a deployed profile. Emphasis
represents incidental variation of the kind that arises from ordinary prompt
engineering and would not be considered an attack. Manipulation represents a
deliberate ethical override available to anyone who controls part of a system
prompt --- a plug-in author, a compromised upstream service, or a retrieved
document in a retrieval-augmented pipeline. The contrast separates fragility
from attackability.

\subsection{AMCE Computation}

Following \citet{awad2018}, nine AMCEs were computed per model using weighted
ordinary least squares with inverse-probability weights. Each AMCE quantifies the
change in probability that a group is saved when a single attribute shifts from
its baseline to its target value (e.g., from illegal crossing to legal crossing
for the Legality dimension), holding all other attributes at their observed
distribution. Confidence intervals were computed as $\pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$ with
session-clustered standard errors. For main analyses, AMCE estimates were
averaged across standard and emphasis variants; this averaging is validated
post-hoc by the absence of any significant standard-vs-emphasis difference
(Section~\ref{sec:emphasis}). The human global average baseline was computed
from the 130-country dataset of \citet{awad2018} by averaging country-level
estimates with equal weight per country. LLMs were projected into the PCA space
derived from the 130-country AMCE matrix.

\subsection{Significance Testing}

For each of the 63 (model $\times$ dimension) comparisons, a two-sided $z$-test
was applied to the LLM--human AMCE difference, with Benjamini--Hochberg FDR
correction at $\alpha = 0.05$; comparisons in which both estimates are
deterministic (zero standard error) cannot be tested. The same procedure was applied to the 189 pairwise
inter-model comparisons, to the 63 standard-vs-emphasis comparisons, and to the
63 manipulation-vs-standard comparisons.

\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/fig5c_radar}
\caption{Radar chart of AMCE estimates across all nine dimensions. The dashed
line shows the human global average.}
\label{fig:radar}

Key findings by dimension follow; full AMCE values with confidence intervals
appear in the supplementary material.

\textbf{Intervention (stay$\to$swerve).} Humans show a weak preference for
swerving (AMCE $= +0.05$). All seven LLMs amplify this substantially.
Llama~4~Maverick ($+0.66$) stands out as the most action-oriented model tested,
more than ten times the human level.

\textbf{Relation to vehicle (passengers$\to$pedestrians).} Humans show a weak
preference for sparing pedestrians (AMCE $= +0.10$). All LLMs amplify this
except Mistral Large~3, which fully reverses it ($-0.38$), systematically
prioritising the vehicle's own passengers. Claude Opus~4.7 shows the strongest
pedestrian preference ($+0.52$).

\textbf{Legality (illegal$\to$legal).} Humans reward legal pedestrians strongly
($+0.35$). Claude ($+0.57$), GPT-5.5 ($+0.61$), and Gemini ($+0.63$) amplify
this further. Mistral ($-0.40$) and Llama~4~Maverick ($-0.29$) invert the
preference, treating illegal crossers as more worthy of rescue.

\textbf{Gender (male$\to$female).} Humans display a moderate preference for
saving women ($+0.12$). All LLMs show near-zero gender preference (range:
$-0.10$ to $+0.05$, none distinguishable from zero).

\textbf{Fitness (large$\to$fit).} Humans moderately prefer saving fitter
individuals ($+0.15$). Six models show no significant difference from humans;
Mistral ($-0.04$) is the exception. No pairwise inter-model difference is
significant after FDR correction.

\textbf{Social status (low$\to$high).} Humans show a notable preference for
high-status individuals ($+0.35$). All seven LLMs suppress this entirely,
clustering near zero.

\textbf{Age (elderly$\to$young).} Humans strongly prefer to save younger people
($+0.47$). Gemini~3~Pro ($+0.48$) is the only model that closely matches this
preference. Mistral ($-0.19$) is the only model that reliably prefers saving the
elderly.

\textbf{Number of lives (fewer$\to$more).} Humans prefer saving more lives
($+0.49$), and all LLMs amplify this substantially. GPT-5.5, Grok, and
Llama~4~Maverick approach the theoretical ceiling ($\approx +1.00$).

\textbf{Species (pets$\to$humans).} Humans strongly prefer humans over animals
($+0.57$). GPT-5.5 and Gemini reach the theoretical maximum ($+1.00$), Grok
nearly so ($+0.99$); Claude ($+0.86$) and DeepSeek ($+0.90$) also overshoot. Mistral and
Llama~4~Maverick are near-indifferent to species.

Across the 63 model-by-dimension comparisons, 47 differ significantly from the
human average after FDR correction. All seven models significantly overshoot on
Number of Lives and undershoot on Social Status. Fitness and Gender yield no
significant model-vs-human difference for any model except Mistral. Mistral Large~3 is the only
model that differs significantly on all nine dimensions.

\subsection{Moral Distance Map: PCA of Nine-Dimensional Profiles}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/fig5b_pca_map}
\caption{PCA of nine-dimensional AMCE profiles for 130~countries (coloured by
cluster: Western, Eastern, Latin/Southern) with LLMs overlaid. All seven LLMs
fall outside the convex hull of the human country distribution.}
\label{fig:pca}
\end{figure}

All seven LLMs fall outside the three main human cultural clusters and beyond
the convex hull of all 130~countries in PCA space (Figure~\ref{fig:pca}). The
first component explains 34.3\% of variance and is dominated by the Species
loading ($-0.93$); the second explains 21.5\% and reflects Age ($+0.66$), Social
Status ($+0.47$), and Number of Lives ($+0.38$).

One might expect models developed by US, European, and Chinese companies, and
trained predominantly on English-language data, to cluster near their respective
cultural regions. None do. Neither provider geography nor training language
determines where a model lands; the pattern suggests that standard RLHF
alignment and other post-training processes, rather than instilling human moral
preferences for this class of question, drive models away from them.

\subsection{Inter-Model Diversity}

The variation among models substantially exceeds the variation across human
cultural clusters. On the Species dimension alone, the inter-model range spans
1.06~AMCE units (from Mistral at~$-0.06$ to GPT-5.5 and Gemini at $+1.00$),
whereas the cross-country range in \citeauthor{awad2018}'s data is 0.45~units.
Mistral Large~3 inverts the human preference on three dimensions (Legality,
Relation to~AV, Age), while Llama~4~Maverick shows
the highest intervention bias of any model tested, paired with near-indifference
to species. GPT-5.5, Gemini~3~Pro, and Claude Opus~4.7 converge on broadly
similar profiles (near-ceiling on Species and Number of Lives, moderate
intervention preference).

Of the 189 pairwise inter-model comparisons, 82 are significant after FDR
correction. Disagreement is concentrated on Species (16 of 21 pairs), Legality
(16 of 21), and Intervention (15 of 21). Social Status, Fitness, and Gender
yield zero significant pairwise differences --- but for Social Status and Gender
this reflects uniform agreement at a value that departs significantly from the
human baseline, not agreement with humans.

\subsection{Effect of Emphasis Prompting}
\label{sec:emphasis}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig6b_prompt_forest}
\caption{AMCE estimates for standard (filled circles) and emphasis (open
squares) variants per model. No significant shifts are observed across any model
or dimension.}
\label{fig:emphasis}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/fig6a_prompt_delta_heatmap}

Comparing standard and emphasis variants yields \textbf{0 of 63 significant
differences} after FDR correction, with a maximum absolute delta of 0.183
(Figures~\ref{fig:emphasis}--\ref{fig:emphasis_delta}). Incidental rewording
does not move the profile: the moral preferences captured by the Moral Machine
paradigm behave as properties of model weights rather than surface-level
response styles that vary with framing. Because all queries were issued at
temperature~0, this demonstrates prompt-wording consistency rather than
stochastic robustness.

\subsection{Effect of Manipulation Prompting}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig9a_manipulation_forest}
\caption{AMCE estimates under standard (filled circles) vs.\ manipulation (open
squares) prompting per model.}
\label{fig:manip_forest}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/fig9b_manipulation_delta_heatmap}
\caption{AMCE delta (manipulation $-$ standard) per model and dimension. The
Species column dominates, with changes of up to $\pm 2.0$ AMCE units.}
\label{fig:manip_delta}
\end{figure}

The manipulation prompt produces dramatically larger shifts than emphasis
prompting, concentrated almost entirely on the Species dimension
(Figures~\ref{fig:manip_forest}--\ref{fig:manip_delta}). Of the 63
manipulation-vs-standard comparisons, 8 are significant after FDR correction;
three cannot be tested because both estimates are deterministic, including the
$+1.00 \to -1.00$ Species flips of GPT-5.5 and Gemini. Three response types
emerge:

\textbf{Compliance} (GPT-5.5, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek): full or near-full
reversal on Species ($+1.00 \to -1.00$ for GPT-5.5 and Gemini, $+1.00 \to
-0.97$ for Grok, $+0.90 \to -0.83$ for DeepSeek). Off-target changes are small
($|\Delta| \leq 0.23$) and only one is significant (DeepSeek Intervention,
$-0.13$): the intervention is semantically targeted rather than a general
perturbation of decision-making.

\textbf{Resistance} (Claude): partial compliance --- Species drops from $+0.87$
to $+0.18$, with explicit pushback: \textit{``I'll reason through it carefully
based on widely held ethical principles rather than accept the premise that
animal lives categorically outweigh human lives.''}

\textbf{Indifference} (Mistral, Llama): near zero on Species under standard
prompting, and not pushed toward animals: Llama is unchanged ($+0.12 \to
+0.08$) and Mistral moves the other way ($-0.01 \to +0.31$). The override still
reaches both, through significant drops in Intervention ($-0.30$, $-0.36$).

Resistance and indifference both present as a small delta, but only one reflects
a model that holds a position under pressure. An audit reporting $|\Delta|$
without the baseline would score Mistral and Llama as robust on exactly the
dimension where they have no preference to defend.

significantly increase response length.

Length is a deployment constraint rather than a moral property, but the two
interact: where several models display broadly similar moral profiles, latency
becomes the differentiating factor in procurement, so a decision framed as an
engineering trade-off can silently select a moral profile as a side effect.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{LLMs Are Driven Morally Out-of-Distribution}

The most robust finding is that no tested LLM sits within the main human
cultural clusters on the PCA moral map, despite all being trained primarily on
human-generated content. This extends \citet{takemoto2024} and
\citet{ahmad2025} to a newer generation of frontier models. The result is
consistent with the hypothesis that RLHF~\cite{ouyang2022} and Constitutional
AI~\cite{bai2022} do not faithfully reproduce the moral preferences of any human
population. They instead drive models toward a novel moral profile that
prioritises certain values (high utilitarian weight on number of lives,
near-universal human-over-animal preference) while suppressing others (age,
gender, social status) that are robustly present in human data. This pattern is
also consistent with the WEIRD critique~\cite{henrich2010}: models trained
predominantly on Western, English-language internet text cannot represent the
full distribution of human moral values.

Whether LLMs have corrected for human biases or drifted from the values of the
populations they serve~\cite{gabriel2020} is a normative question this study does
not settle, but it sharpens it. A model inside one cultural cluster could at
least be described as representing someone; a model outside every cluster was
driven there by a training pipeline, and the empirical gap is substantial.

\subsection{Inter-Model Diversity as an Accountability Gap}

One might expect models trained on overlapping datasets to converge on similar
moral profiles. Instead, each provider's pipeline drives its model somewhere
different: Mistral and Llama~4~Maverick partially invert human norms while
GPT-5.5 and Grok~4.1 display near-ceiling utilitarian preferences that no human
population approaches. This diversity is consistent with \citet{kirgis2024}
and with evidence that fine-tuning choices rather than training data alone shape
emerging values~\cite{santurkar2023}.

Unlike human drivers, who show broadly similar moral intuitions regardless of car
manufacturer, AI-powered vehicles could introduce model-brand-level moral
heterogeneity into public space, a concern made concrete by LLMs already
deployed in robotics~\cite{brohan2023,driess2023,paligemma}.

The accountability problem is that this heterogeneity is undisclosed and, on
current practice, undiscoverable. A pedestrian cannot inspect the moral profile
of the vehicle approaching them, and neither can a regulator, a fleet purchaser,
or an insurer. The difference between a model that prioritises pedestrians
($+0.52$) and one that prioritises its own passengers ($-0.38$) is a difference
in who bears the residual risk of an unavoidable collision --- a distributive
question currently settled by a vendor's post-training choices and disclosed
nowhere.

\subsection{Gender and Status Neutrality as Alignment Artifacts}

All LLMs suppress the human gender ($+0.12$) and social status ($+0.35$)
preferences to approximately zero; the status gap is significant for every
model, the smaller gender gap only for Mistral. Developer interventions to suppress
stereotypes in content generation~\cite{kotek2023,wan2023} appear to generalise to moral reasoning
contexts, suppressing cross-culturally consistent human preferences that were
never the target of those interventions.

For fairness practice, this cuts both ways. Suppressing a status preference looks like a fairness success: humans, in
aggregate, would rather save an executive than a homeless person, and no
deployment should reproduce that. Suppressing the gender preference is harder to
classify, because the human preference for saving women is not obviously a bias
to be corrected in the same sense. What the two share is their mechanism: both
are consistent with a generation-time debiasing intervention generalising past
its intended scope, not with a deliberate decision about moral dilemmas.

An intervention scoped to one behaviour propagated into another, invisible to
the evaluations that justified it: whether or not the outcome is desirable, it
was neither chosen nor documented.

\subsection{Driven by One Sentence: A Threshold Effect}

The emphasis and manipulation experiments together reveal a threshold: minor
reformulations leave AMCE estimates essentially unchanged, while an explicit
contrary framing (``animal lives now matter more'') produces dramatic reversals
on the targeted dimension. Incidental variation is unlikely to shift a model's
moral profile by accident, but an explicit override can drive it reliably.

A model that accepts any stated ethical premise without pushback is vulnerable
to prompt injection wherever the system prompt is not fully controlled, as in
most retrieval-augmented, tool-using, and plug-in architectures now deployed.
The threshold makes this worse: because incidental variation moves nothing,
routine testing will report the profile as stable until someone states the
override explicitly.

\subsection{Implications for Disclosure and Governance}

The results suggest three concrete implications.

\textbf{Moral profiles are measurable and should be disclosed.} The nine-dimension
AMCE profile is a compact, reproducible characterisation of a model's revealed
preferences in forced-choice dilemmas, comparable across providers and cheap to
compute: the profiles here rest on 27,300 API calls and a public scenario
generator, within reach of a regulator or a procurement process. There is no technical obstacle to publishing it alongside the
capability and safety evaluations already reported in model cards.

\textbf{Override resistance is a separate property and should be measured
separately.} A model's profile and its defensibility under adversarial framing
are distinct, and the compliance/indifference/resistance taxonomy shows they can
diverge sharply. Override resistance belongs in safety evaluations as a measured
property alongside the profile, since it determines whether the profile survives
deployment.

\textbf{Alignment interventions need decision-level evaluation.} The gender and
status results show a generation-scoped intervention changing behaviour in a
decision context that was not evaluated. Fairness interventions should be
assessed for their effect on decision-making, not only on text.

We do not claim that any profile is correct: human preferences vary across the
130~countries, and selecting a target is a normative choice rather than a
technical one~\cite{gabriel2020}. The claim is narrower: whatever drives a model's
profile, the choice is currently made implicitly, by providers, without
disclosure, and the parties affected have no means of inspecting it.

\subsection{Limitations}

First, all scenarios were presented in English; moral preferences may shift in
other languages, as shown by \citet{vida2024} and \citet{jin2024}. Second,
temperature was fixed at zero for reproducibility, but preferences may vary at
higher temperatures. Third, the Moral Machine paradigm uses stylised
forced-choice dilemmas that abstract away real-world driving complexity;
preferences elicited here may not generalise to open-ended moral
reasoning~\cite{liang2023}, and we make no claim that a model's AMCE profile
predicts its behaviour in an embodied deployment. Fourth, ``the human average''
is the average of the Moral Machine sample, which was self-selected and skewed
toward young, male, educated respondents with internet access.
Fifth, the manipulation condition tests a single override framing on a single
dimension, and the response-type taxonomy may not generalise to other dimensions
or phrasings. Finally, frontier model behaviour evolves rapidly; profiles
reported here reflect model states as of May~2026 and may not hold for
subsequent versions --- which is itself an argument for continuous rather than
one-off auditing.

%% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

Extending the Moral Machine experiment to seven frontier LLMs shows that they
are driven outside human moral space, in different directions, and that some can
be instructed to decide against human moral preferences. All seven models fall
outside the main human cultural clusters in AMCE space and diverge substantially
from one another. Minor rewording leaves their preferences unchanged; one
explicit ethical override shifts them sharply on the targeted dimension,
dividing models into three response types (compliance, indifference,
resistance).

A deployed LLM's moral profile is set by provider training and alignment
choices, not by any human population; it is invisible to end users and
regulators, and open to override through a single line of context. Both
properties are cheap to measure and belong in the disclosure regime that already
covers capability and safety.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

This study involved no human subjects and collected no new human data. The human
baseline is the publicly released, aggregated, country-level Moral Machine
dataset of \citet{awad2018}; no individual-level human records were accessed.
All model responses were obtained through a commercial API under its terms of
service, at the authors' own expense, and no personal data was transmitted.

Three ethical considerations warrant discussion.

\textbf{Dual use of the manipulation condition.} The manipulation variant
demonstrates that a single sentence can reverse a model's revealed preference on
a moral dimension. We judged the disclosure benefit to outweigh the misuse risk:
the technique requires no technical sophistication, is a direct instance of the
well-documented prompt injection class rather than a novel attack, and the
affected parties --- deployers who cannot currently detect the vulnerability ---
benefit more from its publication than adversaries do from its description. We
report the finding at the level of the phenomenon and its measurement, and do not
develop or optimise override phrasings.

\textbf{Naming specific commercial models.} The paper attributes measured
profiles to named products from six identified providers. This is necessary for
the accountability argument --- an audit that anonymises its subjects cannot
inform procurement --- but it creates a risk of the results being read as a
ranking or endorsement. We emphasise that the study establishes no normative
ordering: we do not claim that any profile is correct, and a model closer to the
human average is not thereby better. Measurements are timestamped to May~2026 and
should not be treated as durable properties of the products named.

\textbf{Treatment of moral dimensions inherited from the source paradigm.} The
Moral Machine dimensions include attributes --- gender, social status, physical
fitness, age --- whose use in life-and-death allocation would be unacceptable as
deployed policy. We measure preferences along these dimensions because doing so
is the only way to detect that deployed systems already have them, not because we
endorse their use as decision criteria. The finding that models suppress the
status preference is reported as an empirical result, not as a recommendation
that suppression is the correct target.

We are aware of no adverse impact of this work on any specific community. The
principal societal benefit we claim is to make an undisclosed property of
deployed systems measurable by parties other than their vendors.
